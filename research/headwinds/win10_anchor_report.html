Forecast review · August 2026 cycle

Did we size the Windows 10 headwind right?

We have trimmed the desktop headwind anchor four times by judgement. This is the first time anyone has held it against telemetry. The data settles less than we might hope — and more than we might like.

Read-only review of data-official/2026-08/adjustments/headwind.json. No specs changed, no forecasts re-run. Legacy telemetry only.

The short answer

Two findings, and they point in different directions.

The data cannot choose between the candidates. Across the stretch of the ramp that has already elapsed, −1,295,000 and −1,270,000 differ by about 11,000 daily users. My measurement carries an uncertainty near 1,500,000. Choosing one over the other is a judgement, and we should call it that rather than dress it as evidence.

Every candidate nevertheless asks for a loss that has not arrived. The ramp starts on 1 April, so by 22 July it has run 112 of its 258 days — 43 percent. A −1,245,000 anchor therefore implies that roughly 540,000 daily users have already gone. I ran ninety variations of the measurement. Not one of them found that loss. The gloomiest found 388,000; the middle of the pack found a small gain.

The problem, then, is not the last 25,000. The problem is the shape.

What the ramp claims

Five lines of JSON, and a strong assumption hiding in them

The spec draws a straight line from zero on 1 April 2026 to its anchor on 15 December 2026, and the applier subtracts that line from the desktop forecast. The anchor stands for people who abandon Firefox rather than carry it from Windows 10 to Windows 11.

A straight line makes a strong claim: the loss accrues evenly, day after day. That claim is testable, because 43 percent of the line already lies in the past. Whatever the anchor's eventual size, 43 percent of it should be visible in telemetry now. Everything below tests exactly that.

A falling Windows 10 curve is not a headwind

The trap the analysis has to step around

When someone upgrades from Windows 10 to Windows 11 they keep Firefox, and our DAU does not move. Migration shuffles users between two buckets; it does not empty them. So the Windows 10 curve, which is falling steeply, measures migration far more than it measures loss. The headwind is attrition — the people who walk away instead of upgrading — and attrition shows up only in the two buckets added together.

The numbers make the trap concrete. Between 1 April and 22 July, Windows 10 shed 1,814,214 daily users. Read that as the headwind and you would size the anchor near 1.8 million per four months. But Windows 11 — supposedly the destination — lost 957,418 over the same stretch, because summer drags on everything, and the two together lost 2,771,631. Mac and Linux, which have no exposure to Windows 10 at all, fell 7.75 percent against the combined cohort's 7.15 percent.

That last comparison contains the entire difficulty of this exercise.

Why the forecast cannot answer this on its own

The model's modern_windows segment is defined as os_version LIKE '%windows 1%', so it holds Windows 10 and Windows 11 together. The forecast parquet cannot separate them. Every split in this note therefore comes from BigQuery.

Finding a clean measurement

Four obstacles stood between the raw extract and an answer

1. Summer swamps the signal

April to July is the worst window in the year for this question. Every cohort falls by a similar percentage, including cohorts with no Windows 10 exposure whatsoever. The raw combined decline of 2,771,631 is almost entirely seasonal, which makes the naive before-and-after difference useless. Any honest estimate has to be a difference against a counterfactual, not a difference against zero.

2. Iran and China had to come out

Two countries carried large one-off shocks inside precisely this window, and neither has anything to do with Windows 10. Iran's telemetry returned after the shutdown, adding 632,573 daily users to the Windows cohort. China's MozillaOnline migration added 755,060. Together they contribute 1,387,633 — more than twice the loss I was trying to detect, and pointing the opposite way. Left in, they would have buried the answer completely.

3. Two tailwinds already sit in the actuals

Launch-on-login and the MozillaOnline overlay both lift the observed figures, so both had to be removed before any comparison — 222,723 daily users in total. Two details surfaced while I did this. The live l spec points at the 180K curve, not the 165K I had been told; the baseline forecast's sidecar SHA1 confirms it. And launch-on-login's measurement window closed permanently on 23 June at 130,296, so its value in July is itself an extrapolation. I carried that as uncertainty rather than pretending the curve was measured.

4. A mistake of my own

My first attempt compared a Wednesday-only average for 2025 against an all-week 28-day average for 2026. Wednesdays run about 13 percent above a full week, so the two were never comparable as levels. I redid the estimate with 2026 measured in all-week terms and 2025 contributing only a ratio, which is unit-free and survives the mismatch. The correction moved the answer, which is why it is worth recording.

What the data shows

Three approaches, none of which finds the loss

The difference against two counterfactuals

I compared the Windows cohort against the same population one year earlier, before Windows 10 end-of-support, and against Mac and Linux over the same months of 2026. The two counterfactuals are independent of each other and rest on different assumptions.

All three land near −7.8 percent. The cohort exposed to Windows 10 is not falling faster than either counterfactual, and the excess attrition — the quantity the anchor is supposed to capture — rounds to nothing. Of the two controls, Mac and Linux is the cleaner: same year, same calendar, no cross-year and no cross-method step.

Ninety variations

A small difference between two large seasonal movements is fragile, so I swept the specification rather than trusting one version of it: both counterfactuals, window endpoints moved a fortnight each way, and all three launch-on-login curves. Ninety variants in total.

The envelope runs from −388,058 to +1,100,235, with a median of +134,926. Every candidate anchor needs something near −540,000. Nothing in the sweep reaches it.

A check that needs no control group

Both estimates above depend on a counterfactual, so I wanted one that does not. Year-over-year change uses the same population twelve months apart and needs no control cohort at all. If Windows 10 attrition were biting, this rate would deteriorate as end-of-support pressure accumulated.

It does not. The rate stood at −5.77 percent when the ramp began and −4.99 percent at the end of the window — 0.78 points better. A real −1,245,000 anchor requires it to have worsened by 1.39 points.

That dip matters, and I would rather raise it than have someone find it later. On one date in early June the rate did touch the level a real anchor implies. The series swings between −2.78 and −7.15 percent over six months, a range wider than the 1.39-point effect under test. So this check cannot rule the headwind out on any particular day. What it shows is the absence of any sustained deterioration — and it shows that the answer's sign depends on where you stop measuring.

What an abandoned cohort actually looks like

Windows 7, 8, and 8.1 give us a natural picture of Firefox decaying on a version the world has left behind. That cohort shed 15.9 percent across our window, against the Windows 10 and 11 pair's 6.6 percent, and it shed a near-identical 15.3 percent in the same months of 2025. Two things follow. An abandoned Windows cohort loses roughly twice what our test cohort loses, and our test cohort is not drifting toward that fate.

This also explains why I excluded Windows 7/8/8.1 from the headline range. Used as a seasonal counterfactual it implies an absurd result — that the combined cohort should have lost nearly four million — because it is not a healthy population having a normal summer. It is a bound, not a control.

What I cannot claim

The limits are real, and they are the most important part of this note

  • Windows 10 attrition does not separate cleanly from ordinary desktop decline. My claim is narrow and negative: the exposed cohort shows no excess decline against its own prior-year season or against Mac and Linux. That is not a claim that attrition is zero.
  • A flat year-over-year rate reads two ways. Either attrition is absent, or it is steady and already embedded in the roughly 5 percent annual decline Prophet trains on. Under the second reading the correct additional headwind is still near zero, because Prophet already carries the embedded rate forward. The anchor earns its place only if attrition accelerates beyond that. Four months of data show no acceleration — and rule out no future acceleration either. That part is a genuine forecast, and no amount of telemetry will settle it.
  • The interval is wide and it is endpoint-sensitive. This is why I offer a range and no point estimate. A single number here would look authoritative and be fiction.
  • The scope is not an exact match. I measured the Windows cohort excluding Iran and China; the anchor applies to total desktop DAU. The mechanism is Windows-specific, so this is the right place to look, but the mapping is approximate.

A second problem: the seam

Raised for the record, not fixed

The applier adds the ramp only from the forecast start onward, and it adds the value the ramp has already reached. So the ramp reads zero on 27 July and −569,419 on 28 July. The composite takes a 569,419 step down on the first day of the forecast.

This misfires under either reading of the evidence. If the April-to-July loss were sitting in the training actuals, Prophet would have fitted it into the level at the seam, and subtracting the ramped value again would double-count it. This analysis finds the loss is not in the actuals — so the step instead asserts a 569,000 overnight drop with no empirical support at all. Either way it is not something telemetry justifies.

Worth noting: the brief cited roughly −592,000 for this step, which corresponds to the superseded −1,295,000 anchor.

What follows

The evidence does not tell us to trim the anchor by another 25,000, and it does not tell us to prefer −1,270,000 over −1,295,000. On that question it is silent, and five cycles of attenuation have been silent judgement dressed as inference.

What the evidence does tell us is that the ramp asserts a past that did not happen. If we want to keep the December magnitude on forward-looking grounds — a bet that abandonment accelerates as Windows 10 decays — that bet deserves to be argued on its own terms and recorded as a bet. Moving the ramp's start toward the forecast seam would let us hold that view without also claiming 540,000 users have already left, and it would remove the day-one step as a side effect.

The narrower recommendation is about how we talk. Each attenuation has been recorded as though data drove it. Two of these numbers differ by less than one percent of the uncertainty around them. Calling that a judgement costs us nothing and keeps the forecast honest.
